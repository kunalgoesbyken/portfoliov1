<Section kicker="For everyone" title="What it does">

Speech models learn from whatever audio you feed them. Silence, hiss, distorted peaks, phone-line artefacts and test tones all end up in the training set unless someone takes them out, and nobody can listen to thousands of hours by hand.

AeroSieve does that listening. Point it at a folder of recordings and it cuts each file into speech clips of 3 to 30 seconds, measures every clip for noise, loudness, distortion and tonality, and keeps only the clips that pass.

Every rejected clip gets a plain reason: `low_snr`, `clipping`, `tonal`, `too_quiet`, `too_loud` or `silence`. You can see what your data actually looked like and loosen a threshold when your recordings are naturally noisy, instead of trusting a black box.

I built it for teams training speech recognition and voice AI, especially in languages where cleaned public data is scarce. I tested it on English, Hindi, Tamil, Bengali, Telugu and Marathi.

The output is ready to train on: WebDataset shards, a manifest of every kept clip and a stats file. The same engine runs as a command-line tool, a Rust library, a Python package, a live WebSocket service for calls and voice agents, and in the browser, where your audio never leaves the page.

</Section>

<Section kicker="Proof" title="Does it hold up on real audio?">

A benchmark I designed can only show that AeroSieve catches the damage I thought of. The question a speech team actually asks is different: are the clips it throws away really worse for a model?

I took 2,917 utterances of Gram Vaani Hindi, 7.8 hours of spontaneous speech recorded over phones in Bihar, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh. Humans checked these transcripts twice, so when a model gets a clip wrong, the audio is to blame, not the label. AeroSieve judged every utterance, and two unrelated recognizers transcribed all of them with no fine-tuning.

| | Whisper-large-v3 | MMS-1B |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Error rate on clips AeroSieve keeps | 55.7% | 52.0% |
| Error rate on clips it rejects | 64.6% | 60.6% |
| Gap, with 95% confidence interval | +8.9 points (+2.2 to +16.9) | +8.6 points (+5.5 to +11.6) |
| Gap within 8 kHz phone audio only | +7.6 points | +6.6 points |

Rejected audio is harder for both models, and the gap survives when only phone recordings are compared, so it is not just "phone calls are harder".

**The test found a bug.** Before the fix, AeroSieve rejected 38% of the 8 kHz recordings as tonal. Low-bitrate codecs cut speech off near 3 kHz, and that empty strip at the top of the band made speech look like a tone. Flatness now ignores band edges the recording does not carry. Kept audio went from 75% to 93%, and every benchmark score stayed identical.

**It also showed what does not work.** The noise check, the one I expected to matter most, did not predict errors here. Its rejections were clips with background music that both models handled fine, and the noise estimate barely tracked errors within a channel. That is now written into the README's limitations rather than left for someone else to discover.

What this does not prove yet is that training on the kept audio gives a better model. That needs a training run comparing raw, filtered and randomly trimmed data, which is planned in the repo.

It ran on free Kaggle GPUs in about 45 minutes. The scripts, full tables and the plan are in [experiments/asr-hi](https://github.com/kunalgoesbyken/AeroSieve/tree/master/experiments/asr-hi).

</Section>

<Section kicker="For engineers" title="How I built it">

A Rust workspace of ten crates, plus two binding crates (`bindings/python`, `bindings/wasm`) built separately. The `aerosieve` library crate wires the stages together: `Pipeline` writes shards, `Inspector` only judges, and `LiveInspector` judges audio that arrives in pieces. `aerosieve-cli` (clap) and `aerosieve-serve` (axum) are thin front ends over it, and `aerosieve-lexical` normalizes transcripts from YAML rules.

<Diagram name="aerosieve-pipeline" title="Pipeline" />

1. **Decode** (`aerosieve-decode`): Symphonia reads wav, mp3, flac, ogg or mp4, downmixes, and rubato resamples to 16 kHz. Loudness is nudged toward -20 dBFS with at most 3 dB of gain, and the source's own full scale is kept so clipping can still be measured.
2. **Segment** (`aerosieve-segment`): a `SpeechDetector` gives each 32 ms window a speech probability. The default `EnergyDetector` uses signal level and needs no model. A hysteresis state machine, modelled on Silero's speech timestamps, ends a clip only after 500 ms of quiet. Clips under 3 s are dropped and longer ones are cut at their deepest internal dip, to at most 30 s.
3. **Score** (`aerosieve-acoustic`): `score` returns four numbers with no ML model, WADA-SNR, RMS level, clip ratio and spectral flatness over the part of 300 to 3400 Hz the recording carries. `FilterPolicy::evaluate` then returns the first failing `RejectReason`, so thresholds can change without re-reading audio.
4. **Shard** (`aerosieve-shard`): accepted clips become `.wav` and `.json` pairs in `shard-*.tar` files, built in a staging directory and renamed into place, with `manifest.jsonl` and `stats.json`. Rejected clips aren't written, but their reasons are counted in the run totals and printed by `aerosieve inspect`.

`Pipeline::process_files` inspects one batch of files per round with rayon and writes results in input order, so a parallel run matches a single-threaded one. `Segmenter::segment` is the `StreamSegmenter` fed the whole file.

The live service exposes `/v1/stream`. Clients send 16-bit PCM frames over a WebSocket, each session holds a `LiveInspector`, and a verdict goes back once the pause that ends a clip is heard. Sessions (256 by default), utterance length, frame size and idle time are capped, and `/metrics` serves Prometheus text with a decision-latency histogram. The browser build compiles the same crates to WebAssembly: an `Inspection` decodes and scores once, then re-applies any `FilterPolicy` to the stored measurements.

<Highlights>
<Highlight title="Checked against real recognizers, not just my own benchmark" result="Rejected clips: +8.9 points WER for Whisper, +8.6 for MMS">
Scoring pre-cut utterances needed a new mode, `score_whole`, because re-segmenting them would separate audio from its transcript and silently drop anything under 3 s. With that in place, two pretrained models transcribed 2,917 human-verified Hindi phone utterances, and the error gap was measured with bootstrap confidence intervals, within each channel as well as overall.
</Highlight>
<Highlight title="A benchmark that can't grade itself" result="24,385 of 24,409 defects caught; the right reason named for all but 2">
SieveBench damages clean speech in 28 known ways (six noise levels, clipping, near silence, tones, hum, phone line) and runs 51,352 damaged copies through the same code as the CLI. The expected verdict comes from the damage, never from AeroSieve's thresholds, so tuning can't make the benchmark agree with itself.
</Highlight>
<Highlight title="The benchmark found real bugs" result="Kept Hindi speech rose from 16.4 to 25.6 minutes (+56%)">
Loudness leveling was hiding clipping, so distorted files passed as clean. Clipping is now measured against the source's full scale. Speech was also getting cut at every 100 ms word gap, which left 23 of 73 clean English recordings with no clip at all. Pauses under 500 ms are now bridged.
</Highlight>
<Highlight title="One state machine for live and batch" result="1,000 concurrent streams on a 4-core laptop, 0 failures">
Batch processing is the streaming segmenter fed the whole file, so live and batch verdicts can't disagree. A property test streams in random chunk sizes and checks the segments match, and a live test checks clips and scores match exactly across several chunk sizes. The live path judges samples as received, with no loudness leveling.
</Highlight>
<Highlight title="Noise measured without a clean reference" result="Within about 1.3 dB from 0 to 20 dB SNR">
WADA-SNR estimates noise from the amplitude distribution of the clip alone. Babble reads up to 2.7 dB too clean, which I documented as a limitation instead of hiding it. Flatness skips bins near Nyquist, where resampled audio is empty, and band edges more than 30 dB below the band's median, where a codec has cut the speech off.
</Highlight>
<Highlight title="Fast by default" result="43 minutes of 48 kHz Hindi in 2.75 s, about 940x real time">
Files are inspected in batches across every core with rayon and written back in input order. Unpaced, the server pushed 1.27 hours of audio through in 0.6 seconds.
</Highlight>
<Highlight title="The real pipeline in the browser" result="A 70 second file decoded and judged in 148 ms, client side">
Decode, segment and score compile to a 1.7 MB WebAssembly module. The demo stores each clip's measurements and re-applies the policy when a slider moves, so nothing is decoded twice.
